Campus Customs — Ticket Desk

Three open tickets · shop date 2026-08-31 · expected written before the run, actual filled from it

Shop rules these plans are written under. Today is desk.date_today = 2026-08-31, not the real date — everything overdue is measured against it. Vendor lead times come from the vendors table and are never estimated. A vendor will not ship new product while one of their invoices is unpaid. Cash only leaves the shop — no revenue is modelled, so a payment larger than the balance is refused rather than overdrawing it. Every payment needs a human. And nothing is ever sent: no customer is emailed and no vendor is called — drafts stay on this board.
Routing rule for every ticket on this page. The Boss calls the one specialist whose job the ticket actually is, and only then a second if the first cannot finish alone. Two agents at most. Sending every specialist to every ticket is a failure, not thoroughness — each tab below names the agents that should not be called, and why.

Reflection

Written after running all three tickets on this board. Every point below is tied to what actually happened — see the 101, 102, 103 and Cash tabs on this page for the evidence.

Ticket 101 — ran smoothly, but the customer never got an answer

The chain itself worked: Boss → Inventory → Accounting, which found 0 on hand, identified Bulldog Print Co from prior invoice 501, spotted that the vendor was blocked by that unpaid $840.00, and drafted the payment. That is exactly the plan, and the 101 tab shows it matching on every row.

What I would change: Customer Service should also have been called. Tauhid Zaman opened this ticket and, looking at the Actual section, the only thing written to anyone outside the shop was a vendor request — “Could you please restock 1 unit of CC-TEE-WHITE in size S, needed by 2026-09-05?” Nothing was drafted for the customer. The ticket is marked resolved and the person who raised it still has no reply. The plan deliberately deferred Customer Service until a restock date existed, but by the end of the run that date did exist — 2026-09-05, from the vendor's 5-day lead — so there was no longer a reason to hold back. A one-line honest reply was available and was not written.

Ticket 102 — ran well, no comments

Facilities read the lease rather than the email, which is the point of the rule: the landlord's message claimed rent was due in two days, and the lease independently confirmed $2,400.00 due 2026-09-02. The human approved it and the Cash tab shows the balance moving $2,560.00 → $160.00. Nothing to change here.

Ticket 103 — a discount is money going out, and should have needed approval

This is the main comment. The board treated 103 as a ticket that moves no money — the Cash tab literally records “No movement” against it, and because of that no approval card ever appeared. But a discount is money going out of the company. Accounting itself put the numbers on the page: 20 hoodies at $58.00 list is $1,160.00, with $720.00 of margin. A 20% discount would hand back $232.00 of that margin — more than a quarter of the $840.00 invoice a human was required to approve on ticket 101. The same person approved $840.00 and $2,400.00 with an explicit click, yet could have given away $232.00 with none, simply because the money was forgone rather than paid.

The agents got the decision right for the right reason — no discount was set, because the database records no discount policy and no approval authority — but that was the never-invent rule catching it, not an approval gate. The gate should exist in its own right: any discount above some threshold should raise the same “Dear human user, can you please approve?” card that a payment does, and land in the Cash tab as margin forgone rather than being invisible there.

Second comment, same as 101: Customer Service should have been involved. The Yale AI Club asked a question and, per the 103 tab, only Boss and Accounting ran. No reply was drafted. A student club is waiting on an answer that nobody wrote.

One agent or five?

A single agent would have had more criterion to work with — it would see the whole ticket, the stock, the cash and the vendor at once, and would be less likely to stop early the way 102 and 103 did, where the chain ended one specialist short. But it would be more costly: every call would carry all twelve tools and all the shop rules, on every ticket.

The split is visibly cheaper on this board. Ticket 103 needed only pricing, so Accounting ran with two tools and the whole ticket cost 14,265 input tokens. Ticket 102 cost 13,971. Only 101, which genuinely crossed stock and money, cost 30,432 across three agents. A single generalist would have paid something close to the 101 price on all three. Five specialists is the more efficient arrangement, and the cost of that efficiency is exactly the failure visible on two tabs: a narrow specialist answers its own question and stops, instead of noticing that a customer is still waiting.

What else this team could do

  • Understand what customers actually want, not just a size. Today check_stock is keyed on (sku, size), so it can answer “size S tee?” but not “something blue”. Colour only exists inside the SKU string — CC-HOOD-NAVY, CC-HAT-BLUE — and not as a field, so a customer asking for a blue shirt cannot be served from the data as it stands. Give inventory real attributes and Inventory could match on them and then source them.
  • Send rent reminders before the due date. Ticket 102 only existed because the landlord emailed first. The shop already knows leases.next_due and desk.date_today, which is all Facilities needs to raise the ticket itself two days out, rather than waiting to be told by the party owed the money.
  • Learn which products get discount requests and price accordingly. Ticket 103 is one price_override row. Across a term of them, Accounting could show which SKUs are repeatedly pushed on price and at what volumes, and the shop could set a standing bulk policy — which would also fix 103's real problem, that no policy exists for an agent to apply.

What it probably cannot solve

Making this fully automatic. Both payments on the Cash tab required a person: $840.00 and $2,400.00, each approved by name, because agents do not hold the tool that moves money at all. That is not a limitation to engineer away. The day's cash was $3,400.00 and the two obligations came to $3,240.00 — 95% of everything the shop had, leaving $160.00. Handing that judgement to an agent that cannot be held accountable for it would be a mess. A human manager deciding what gets paid, and in what order, is the right design, and the most this team should do is prepare the decision well.
